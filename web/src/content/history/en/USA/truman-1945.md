## History

Harry Truman had been vice-president for only 82 days when Franklin Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945. A former senator from Missouri, he had been kept out of the main decisions of the war and learned of the atomic bomb project only after taking office. Within four months he oversaw the end of the war in Europe, attended the Potsdam Conference with Stalin and Churchill (replaced midway by Clement Attlee), and authorized the use of atomic bombs against Hiroshima and Nagasaki, followed by Japan's surrender.

His nearly eight years in office are usually divided in two. The first, until 1948, was marked by the difficult conversion to a peacetime economy and the gradual breakdown of the wartime alliance with the Soviet Union. The second, after his unexpected victory in the 1948 election, was dominated by the Cold War: the Soviet atomic bomb and the communist victory in China in 1949, the Korean War from 1950, and at home the anti-communist climate that would be known as McCarthyism.

Truman left office in January 1953 deeply unpopular, with the war in Korea at a stalemate. Historians have since revised that judgment, and his presidency is now generally seen as having laid the foundations of US foreign policy for the following four decades.

## Economy

The end of the war posed the risk that unemployment would return as millions of soldiers came home and war production stopped. It did not: savings accumulated during the war, the GI Bill and pent-up demand for consumer goods sustained a boom. The Employment Act of 1946 made "maximum employment" a federal objective and created the Council of Economic Advisers.

The transition was turbulent, however. The lifting of wartime price controls in 1946 brought the highest inflation of the post-war period, and a wave of strikes hit coal, steel, automobiles and the railroads; Truman threatened to draft striking railroad workers into the army. In 1947 the Republican Congress passed the Taft–Hartley Act, restricting union activity, over his veto.

After 1948 Truman proposed the Fair Deal, extending the New Deal with national health insurance, federal aid to education and civil rights legislation. Congress approved a higher minimum wage, an expansion of Social Security and the 1949 Housing Act, but not health insurance. The Korean War brought a new burst of inflation and the return of wage and price controls. In 1952, to avert a strike during the war, Truman seized the steel mills; the Supreme Court ruled the seizure unconstitutional (*Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*), a landmark limit on presidential power.

## Politics

The Republicans won both houses of Congress in 1946, and most observers expected Truman to lose in 1948. His own party split three ways: Southern Democrats opposed to civil rights formed the "Dixiecrat" ticket of Strom Thurmond, and the left followed Henry Wallace's Progressive Party. Truman ran an energetic "whistle-stop" campaign against the "do-nothing" Congress and defeated Thomas Dewey — the result that produced the famous mistaken headline "Dewey Defeats Truman".

On civil rights he went further than any predecessor: he created a presidential committee on civil rights in 1946, and in 1948 ordered the desegregation of the armed forces by Executive Order 9981. Congress, blocked by Southern senators, passed no civil rights law.

The Cold War reshaped domestic politics. In 1947 Truman created a loyalty program for federal employees; from 1950, Senator Joseph McCarthy's accusations of communist infiltration in the government, amplified by the fall of China and espionage cases, put the administration on the defensive. The National Security Act of 1947 reorganized the government for a long confrontation, creating the National Security Council, the CIA and what became the Department of Defense. The 22nd Amendment, limiting presidents to two terms, was ratified in 1951; Truman, who was exempted, chose not to run again in 1952.

## Foreign policy

Truman inherited Roosevelt's plans for a post-war order based on cooperation among the great powers: the United Nations, whose Charter was signed in June 1945, and the Bretton Woods institutions, the IMF and the World Bank. That cooperation broke down quickly over Poland, Germany, Iran and Turkey. George Kennan's "Long Telegram" of 1946 and his 1947 article on "the sources of Soviet conduct" gave the new policy its name: containment.

The Truman Doctrine of March 1947, with aid to Greece and Turkey, turned containment into a global commitment. The Marshall Plan followed, providing about $13 billion to rebuild Western Europe between 1948 and 1952. When the Soviet Union blockaded West Berlin in June 1948, the United States and Britain supplied the city by air until the blockade was lifted in May 1949. In April 1949 the North Atlantic Treaty created NATO, the first peacetime military alliance the United States entered outside the Western Hemisphere. The administration also recognized Israel minutes after its declaration of independence in May 1948 and launched the Point Four program of technical aid to developing countries.

In the Americas, the Rio Treaty of 1947 — signed at a conference closed by Truman, who visited Brazil and addressed its Congress — and the founding of the Organization of American States in Bogotá in 1948 organized the hemisphere for collective defense. Latin American governments, however, received no equivalent of the Marshall Plan, a lasting source of disappointment in the region.

1949 changed the balance: the Soviet Union tested an atomic bomb and the Communists won the civil war in China. Truman approved the development of the hydrogen bomb, and NSC-68 called for a large military build-up. When North Korea invaded the South in June 1950, he committed US forces under a United Nations mandate. After Chinese intervention reversed the advance to the Yalu River, he relieved General Douglas MacArthur in April 1951 for publicly pushing to widen the war — a decisive affirmation of civilian control over the military. Armistice talks began in 1951 but the war was still being fought when he left office.
