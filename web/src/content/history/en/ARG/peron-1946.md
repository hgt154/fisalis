## History

Perón rose to power inside the military regime born of the June 1943 coup. As secretary of labor and later vice-president and war minister, he turned a minor department into the center of a new alliance with the trade unions: collective agreements, pensions, paid holidays and labor courts brought him the loyalty of a fast-growing urban working class. When rivals in the army forced his resignation and detention in October 1945, a mass demonstration in the Plaza de Mayo on 17 October secured his release. It was the founding moment of Peronism.

In the February 1946 election he faced the Unión Democrática, an alliance of radicals, socialists, communists and progressive democrats, with conservative support in some provinces, backed by the former US ambassador, Spruille Braden. Washington's "Blue Book", accusing the Argentine government of collaborating with the Axis, backfired: Perón campaigned on the slogan "Braden or Perón" and won about 53% of the vote. He took office on 4 June 1946.

Over the next nine years, with his wife Eva Perón at his side until her death in 1952, he transformed the Argentine state, the economy and the balance of social power, and divided the country into Peronists and anti-Peronists, a cleavage that would outlast him by decades.

## Economy

Perón inherited large reserves built up during the war and high prices for Argentine grain and meat. The government used them to pursue industrialization and redistribution. Around the transition of 1946 the central bank and bank deposits were nationalized and the IAPI was created, giving the state a monopoly on grain exports: it paid farmers less than world prices and used the margin to finance industry and imports.

The First Five-Year Plan (1947–1951) expanded energy, transport and social spending. On 9 July 1947, in Tucumán, Perón proclaimed a "Declaration of Economic Independence"; the foreign debt was paid off, and foreign-owned utilities were bought, above all the British railways, transferred to the state in 1948 for about £150 million, using sterling balances that Britain had blocked. Real wages rose sharply and the share of wages in national income reached historic highs.

The model ran into limits from 1949. Grain prices fell, the drought of 1951–52 ruined harvests, reserves were exhausted and inflation went above 30% in 1951. Agriculture, squeezed by the IAPI, stagnated. In 1952 the government changed course with an emergency stabilization plan: wage restraint, a campaign to raise productivity and to save, and better prices for farmers. The Second Five-Year Plan (1953–1957) and the 1953 foreign investment law opened the door to foreign capital; the 1955 contract with Standard Oil of California to explore for oil in Patagonia was attacked as a betrayal of the economic nationalism Perón had championed.

## Politics

Peronism was both a mass movement and a state project. The CGT trade union confederation grew into one of the largest in the world and became a pillar of the government. Through the Eva Perón Foundation, Eva channelled social assistance directly to the poor and led the Peronist women's branch; women won the vote in 1947 and voted for the first time in 1951. The 1949 Constitution added social rights and allowed the president's immediate re-election.

The same years saw a steady concentration of power. The Supreme Court was purged through impeachment in 1947; opposition newspapers were restricted, and *La Prensa* was expropriated in 1951; opposition politicians, including the radical leader Ricardo Balbín, were jailed; school curricula and public administration were filled with official propaganda. Historians still debate how to classify the regime: an elected government that won large majorities, but that narrowed the space for opposition as time went on. The 1946 vote is widely regarded as fair; the 1951 one took place with opposition newspapers restricted and opposition leaders jailed.

Re-elected in November 1951 with 62.5% of the vote, Perón began his second term weakened by the economic crisis and by Eva's death in July 1952. In 1954 he entered into conflict with the Catholic Church, a former ally: divorce was legalized (1954) and religious education in state schools abolished (1955). On 16 June 1955, navy aircraft bombed the Plaza de Mayo in a failed coup attempt, killing more than 300 people; that night Peronist groups burned churches in central Buenos Aires. In September a military uprising led by General Eduardo Lonardi began in Córdoba. Facing the threat of a naval bombardment of Buenos Aires, Perón stepped aside and went into exile, which would last eighteen years.

## Foreign policy

Perón called his foreign policy the "Third Position": Argentina would belong to neither the capitalist nor the communist bloc. In practice this meant autonomy within the Western hemisphere rather than neutrality. Argentina established diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union in 1946 and traded with Eastern Europe, but signed the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance in 1947 (ratified in 1950) and joined the Organization of American States.

Relations with the United States swung between conflict and pragmatism. After the Braden episode came a period of restrictions: Argentine historians have long argued that Washington's limits on using Marshall Plan dollars to buy Argentine grain hurt the country's exports, a reading others consider exaggerated. As the economy weakened, Perón sought rapprochement: an Export-Import Bank loan in 1950, the visit of Milton Eisenhower in 1953 and openness to US oil companies. Argentina did not join the IMF and the World Bank, nor send troops to Korea.

In Latin America, Perón promoted economic integration with neighbours: the Act of Santiago with Chile in 1953, similar agreements with Paraguay, Ecuador and others, and the idea of a new "ABC" bloc with Brazil and Chile, which Vargas's Brazil never embraced. The government also backed ATLAS, a Latin American trade union confederation outside both Cold War camps. Towards Europe, Argentina extended credits and grain to Franco's Spain when it was isolated; and in the post-war years a number of Nazi war criminals found refuge in Argentina, a chapter investigated by an official commission in the late 1990s.
